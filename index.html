Operations Case Study

Holding the Line

A part-time concierge shift, a team stuck at 78–85% service level, and no system to catch who'd quietly gone missing from queue. Three years later, that team was routing calls at 93–100% — sustained until the team was decommissioned in a company restructure.

TL;DR

Diagnosed three root causes behind a chronically missed SLA and fixed each one — service level went from 78–85% to a sustained 93–100% over three years.

01

The queue, as I found it

As a part-time concierge — routing calls to the right manager based on what the caller told me — I saw the same three cracks show up every month.

Coverage

Breaks taken all at once

Instead of staggering in batches, people stepped away together, leaving the queue thin or empty at predictable stretches of the day.

ACW

No cap on after-call work

Agents lingered in ACW well past what the task needed, most of it lost to manually drafting emails in Outlook instead of taking the next call.

Adherence

No way to catch a no-show

If someone wasn't logged in where the schedule said they should be, nothing flagged it — the gap only showed up in the SLA number at month's end.

02

What I put in place

When I moved into leadership-in-training and landed the Concierge team, I built five fixes around the actual causes, not the symptom.

01

Staggered break scheduling

Breaks moved to a rotation with a guaranteed minimum staffed on queue at all times, instead of a free-for-all.

Scheduling
02

A hard 1-minute ACW cap

Made after-call work a tracked, enforced number rather than an open-ended pause between calls.

Compliance
03

Standard scripts

Gave the team a faster, consistent way to route and close out common call types.

Enablement
04

Automated the call log → email step

Built a Monday.com form for every call log; when the resolution was "Send Email," it fired the email to the manager automatically — no more hand-typing it in Outlook, the single biggest driver of long ACW.

Automation
05

Coaching & training materials

Built the reference material the team needed to actually hold the new standard, not just be told about it once.

Training
03

The system behind fix 04

The automated call-log board this ran on — every call became a row, and a "Send Email" resolution fired the email automatically instead of someone drafting it by hand in Outlook.

The result

Every fix targeted a specific leak — coverage, ACW, or manual work — rather than asking the team to simply "do better." Once they landed, the SLA didn't just touch 90% once; it stayed above it for over two years straight.

Operations Case Study

The Handoff

When the Concierge team was decommissioned, its strongest agents were set to move into a department handling client meetings and admin work — a real step up from call handling. But those same agents were still carrying workflows for our own clients, so a straight handover risked dropping our side of the business while clients kept operating as usual. Planning and running that transition fell to me.

TL;DR

Planned and ran a 9-step handover so top agents could move to a new department without dropping any of the client workflows they were still carrying.

01

The situation

The upgrade

A real step forward for them

The agents who excelled on calls were headed to a department built around client meetings and administrative work — a meaningful step up from the queue.

The risk

A gap on our side

Those same agents were still handling live workflows for our clients. Move them over without a plan, and our clients feel the disruption while theirs keep running as normal.

02

The transition protocol

Nine steps, run in order, so nothing moved until the person, the workflow, and the replacement were all actually ready.

Plan
01

Scope the transition

Identify the transfer dates, who's actually needed on the other side, and which workflows each person carries.

02

Build the tracking board

One board per person: transfer date, workflows handled, behavior notes, any violations on record, and their metric scorecard.

Align
03

Meet with each agent

Walk them through the transition and find out directly whether they're on board or have concerns.

04

Loop in HR

Hand off anything payroll-related to HR — outside my scope to manage, and kept confidential.

Enable
05

Train the replacement

Run training for the new joiner or whoever's picking up the workflow. I sit in on key sessions to confirm it's landing. If capacity's a concern, the workflow moves to the next person instead.

06

Test for readiness

Check the knowledge actually stuck before letting it near a real client.

07

Practice on live samples

Hands-on runs against real scenarios, not just theory.

Decide
08

Finalize readiness

A clear go / no-go call on whether the handover is actually ready.

09

Make the transfer decision

Confirm per person whether they move or stay.

Operations Case Study

The Upgrade

Most of our SOP documents were in an outdated format, and some were genuinely hard for a new hire to follow without someone walking them through it live. That model works, but it runs entirely on trainer time — time that same trainer also needs for their own workload. The fix wasn't just to rewrite the SOPs; it was to build a self-serve course around them, so new hires could learn independently and trainer time went only where it was actually needed.

TL;DR

Rebuilt outdated SOPs into a self-serve LMS course with quiz checkpoints, freeing trainer time for only the new hires who genuinely needed it.

01

The problem

Format

SOPs stuck in an outdated format

Most of the document library hadn't been touched in a while — the format buried the information a reader actually needed to find.

Onboarding

Hard to follow for someone brand new

Several SOPs assumed context a recently onboarded hire simply didn't have yet, so the document alone couldn't carry the training.

Trainer time

1:1 training doesn't scale

Walking every new hire through the SOP live works, but it competes directly with the trainer's own responsibilities every time onboarding comes up.

02

The options on the table

Two fixes were on the table. The call was to run both, together, rather than pick one.

Option A

Rewrite the SOPs

Modernize the format and clean up the language, so the document itself does more of the explaining without needing a live walkthrough.

Option B

Build an LMS course

Turn onboarding into an interactive course with quizzes built in, so we can actually confirm the material landed instead of assuming it did.

The call

Both, combined — not either

An improved SOP feeds directly into the LMS course, quizzes confirm who's actually ready, and a live "do session" is reserved only for whoever's genuinely stuck — not run as a default for every new hire.

03

The training protocol

A documented handover plan, so onboarding follows the same guidelines every time instead of depending on which trainer happens to run it.

Build
01

Audit and rewrite the SOPs

Modernize the format and clarify the language for someone with zero prior context on the role.

02

Build the LMS course

Turn the SOP into an interactive course, with quizzes built in to check whether the material actually landed.

Learn
03

Self-paced onboarding

The new hire reads the SOP and works through the LMS course independently, before any 1:1 trainer time is spent.

04

Quiz checkpoints

Passing scores confirm understanding; anyone below the bar is flagged for support rather than pushed ahead.

Support

A guided walkthrough or coaching session, reserved for the people who actually need it — not run as a default for everyone.

06

Confirm

08

Once we're confident the person can carry the role unsupervised, they take it on solo.

04

Where trainer time goes

Before

Every new hire, live

The trainer walks each person through the SOP 1:1 — consistent, but it competes with their own workload every single time.

Now

Self-serve first, live when needed

The new hire reads the SOP and clears the LMS course solo. Trainer time goes only to whoever the quiz results flag as needing it.

The pattern holds here too: don't spend a scarce resource — trainer time — on people who don't need it. Build the system that finds out who does.
